Your ball starts left, then peels away to the right and finishes two fairways over. You have
been told to strengthen your grip, keep your head down, swing more from the inside, and buy a
different driver. Some of that advice is right for you. Most of it is right for somebody else.

The reason slice advice is so contradictory is that a slice is not one fault. It is one *ball
flight* produced by two quite different faults, and the fix for one makes the other worse.
Before you change anything, it is worth spending five minutes finding out which one you have.

## What a slice actually is

Under the modern ball flight laws, two things at impact determine everything:

1. **The club face** controls where the ball starts. It begins its flight close to where the face
   is pointing, and the face has more influence the less loft the club has — which is why the
   driver is where slices hurt most.
2. **The difference between the face and the swing path** controls how much the ball curves. If
   the path is left of where the face points, the spin axis tilts right and the ball bends right.

So the technical definition of a slice is precise and useful:

> A slice is a club face that is **open relative to the swing path** at impact. Not open relative
> to the target — open relative to the path.

That distinction is the whole thing. A face pointing 3° right of target with a path 3° right of
target produces a dead straight push, no curve at all. A face pointing 3° *left* of target with a
path 8° left produces a ball that starts left and slices hard, even though the face is closed to
the target.

The face being "open" in everyday terms is not what bends the ball. The gap between face and
path is.

## Confirm it is actually a slice

Three different misses get called a slice, and only two of them are one. Watch five shots and
note where the ball **starts** relative to your target, then which way it **bends**.

| Starts | Bends | What it is | Root cause |
|---|---|---|---|
| Left | Right | **Pull slice** — the classic | Path well left of the face |
| Straight | Right | **Straight slice** | Path left, face happens to match target |
| Right | Right | **Push slice** — the big miss | Face open to target, path left of it |
| Right | None | Push, not a slice | Alignment or ball position |
| Left | None | Pull, not a slice | Alignment or ball position |

If your ball starts offline and flies dead straight, you do not have a slice. You have an aim
problem, and the fixes below will make things worse. The
[ball flight diagnostic](/tools/ball-flight-diagnostic/) walks through this in more detail and
covers all nine flights.

## Which of the two causes is yours

Both produce a slice. They need opposite fixes. Here is the test.

**Take a mid iron and hit five shots, aiming at a specific target. Note where each one starts.**

- **If they start left of target**, your path is the problem. The face is not wildly open — it may
  even be closed to the target — but the club is travelling well left across the ball. This is
  the *path-driven slice*, and it is by far the more common of the two.
- **If they start right of target**, your face is the problem. It is genuinely open at impact and
  the ball is starting where it points. This is the *face-driven slice*.

Do the test with an iron rather than a driver. Driver ball flight exaggerates everything and the
tee height adds another variable.

## Fixing the face-driven slice

Your face is open at impact. The order below matters, because each step removes a reason for the
next problem to exist.

### 1. Check your grip first

Look down at address. You should see **two to three knuckles** on your lead hand. If you see one
or none, your grip is weak and the face will need active manipulation to square up — which is not
something that holds together under pressure.

Rotate both hands slightly away from the target until you see those knuckles. It will feel
strange and it will look, to you, like the face is closed. That feeling is normal and fades in
about a bucket of balls.

### 2. Check the trail hand too

Most golfers adjust the lead hand and stop. The trail hand should sit so that the V between thumb
and forefinger points roughly toward your trail shoulder. If it sits on top of the grip, it will
hold the face open no matter what the lead hand does.

### 3. Let the forearms rotate

A face that cannot close is often a face that is being *held* open by an arm action that resists
rotation. Make slow half swings and feel the trail forearm rotating over the lead one through
impact. You are not flipping your wrists — you are allowing a rotation that a tense grip prevents.

### 4. Get your weight forward

Hanging back on the trail foot leaves the face open almost automatically. If your finish position
has weight on your back foot, that is the fault to fix before anything more sophisticated.

## Fixing the path-driven slice

Your path travels left across the ball. This is the more common cause and, awkwardly, the one
most amateur corrections make worse.

### 1. Check your alignment before your swing

This is not filler advice. Lay a club on the ground pointing at your target and set up to it.
Most golfers who slice discover their shoulders are aimed substantially left — and an out-to-in
path is the only sensible way to hit a ball from that setup. The swing is not broken; it is
solving the problem your aim created.

Fix aim first. A meaningful share of path-driven slices resolve here and nowhere else.

### 2. Stop aiming further left

Here is the trap. The ball finishes right, so you aim more left to compensate. Aiming left makes
the path travel further left, which widens the gap between face and path, which makes the ball
curve *more*. You then aim further left again.

If you have been slicing for years and aiming further left each season, this loop is why.

### 3. Build a feel for an in-to-out path

Place a headcover or an alignment stick just outside the ball, angled slightly, so that an
out-to-in swing would strike it. Make slow swings that miss it. The feedback is immediate and it
does not require you to understand what your body is doing.

Expect the first genuine in-to-out strikes to feel wrong and to go right. That is progress — you
have changed the path, and the face has not caught up yet.

### 4. Start the downswing from the ground

An out-to-in path frequently begins with the upper body starting the downswing, which throws the
club outside the correct line before the arms have any say. Feel the sequence starting with
pressure moving into the lead foot while the arms are still finishing the backswing.

## The order to work in, and why

Do not do all of this at once. Work down the list and stop when the ball flight changes.

1. **Alignment** — free, instant, and the cause more often than anyone expects.
2. **Grip** — one change, then hit twenty balls before judging it.
3. **Ball position** — a fraction back if the strike is late.
4. **Weight forward** — a setup and finish issue before it is a swing issue.
5. **Path work** — only now, and only with feedback like a headcover gate.

The order is deliberate. Every item above the one you need can *create* the fault you are trying
to fix lower down, so working bottom-up means fighting your own setup.

## What about equipment?

A draw-biased driver, extra loft, or a closed hosel setting will genuinely reduce a slice. The
mechanism is real: those designs help the face close relative to the path, which is precisely the
relationship that causes the curve.

Two honest caveats. It reduces the curve rather than removing it — a severe slice will still
slice with a draw-biased head. And it does nothing for your irons, because the fault is still in
the swing. Buy it as a way to enjoy your rounds while you fix the cause, not as the fix.

If you want to go that route, our [equipment guides](/gear/) covers what draw bias and
adjustability actually do.

## What to expect

If your cause was grip or alignment, the ball flight can change in a single session. You are
changing where you stand and how you hold the club, not how you move.

If your cause was an ingrained out-to-in path, give it several weeks of deliberate practice. You
are rebuilding a movement pattern that currently works, in the sense that it repeats. It will get
worse before it gets better, usually via a spell of pulls as the path improves before the face
adjusts. That phase is a sign it is working, not a sign it is not.
