Ask what a golf simulator costs and you will get a number. The number will be wrong, because a
simulator is not a product. It is six purchases, a room, and a running cost, and the six
purchases do not arrive in the same box.

This page gives you the structure instead of the number. Once you can see all the components,
including the ones that never appear in a package price, you can price your own build accurately
and stop comparing quotes that are not describing the same thing.

## Why a single price is always misleading

A package listed at one price and a package listed at four times that price are frequently not
competing products. One is a launch monitor and a net. The other is a launch monitor, a mat, a
screen, an enclosure, a projector, software, and the mounting hardware to hold it together.

Three things make headline prices incomparable:

1. **Packages bundle different component counts.** There is no standard definition of what a
   "complete" simulator package includes. Some include the mat, some do not. Almost none include
   the computer.
2. **The room is treated as free.** It is not. Ceiling height, power, lighting and flooring are
   real costs that fall on you and vary enormously between a finished basement and a bare garage.
3. **Running costs are omitted entirely.** Software subscriptions renew. Mats wear out. Screens
   take a beating from thin shots and eventually need replacing.

> A simulator price is only meaningful alongside the component list it covers and the room it
> assumes. Without both, it is a marketing number.

## The six components

Every simulator build, from the cheapest to the most expensive, is some version of this stack.
You may skip components. You may not skip the decision.

### 1. The launch monitor

The device that measures your shot. This is normally the largest single line item and the widest
price range in the build, because the category spans units that measure two or three values and
model the rest, and units that measure nearly everything directly.

The distinction that matters is not brand or price. It is **which numbers the unit measures and
which it estimates**, and how the unit needs to be positioned relative to the ball. Camera-based
units generally sit behind or beside the ball; radar units generally need space in front of it.
That requirement interacts with your room before it interacts with your budget, which is why
this decision cannot be made from a spec sheet alone. Our
[launch monitor guide](/home-golf/launch-monitors/) covers the measured-versus-estimated split
in detail.

### 2. The hitting mat

The component people under-spend on and regret. A cheap mat is a thin layer of turf over a hard
base, and it does two things badly: it lets the club bounce into the ball on a fat strike, which
hides a fault you need to see, and it transmits the shock of every strike straight into your
wrists and elbows.

If your budget is tight, this is the component to protect at the expense of nearly everything
else. See [hitting mats](/home-golf/hitting-mats/) for what to look for.

### 3. The net or the enclosure

The thing that stops the ball. A freestanding net is the cheap route and works well. An enclosure
is a frame with netting on the sides and top, usually holding the impact screen, and it is the
route you take once a projector is involved.

Both are safety equipment before they are golf equipment. See
[hitting nets](/home-golf/hitting-nets/) for the safety margins that actually matter.

### 4. The impact screen

The surface you project onto and hit into. Screens trade image quality against ball-speed
tolerance and noise. A tighter, smoother screen shows a better picture; a heavier, looser screen
survives more and rebounds less violently. There is no screen that maximises both.

**This is the component to cut first if the budget is short.** Hitting into a net with a launch
monitor gives you every number the screen would have given you. What you lose is the picture.

### 5. The projector and the computer

Two costs, usually counted as one, and frequently counted as zero.

The projector's throw ratio is set by your room, not your preference: a low ceiling or a short
room forces a short-throw or ultra-short-throw unit, which costs more than a standard projector
of the same brightness. And the simulation software has to run somewhere. A laptop that handles
email will not necessarily render a course at a frame rate you can putt on.

### 6. The software

Usually a subscription, occasionally a one-time licence, and frequently locked to your launch
monitor brand. Two things to establish before you buy the hardware: whether the software you want
supports the unit you are considering, and whether the licence renews annually. A renewal you did
not budget for is an unwelcome surprise in year two.

## The costs that sit outside every package price

This is where builds go over budget. None of the following appears in a simulator package
listing, and all of them are real.

| Cost | Why it appears | Who it hits hardest |
|---|---|---|
| **Short-throw projector premium** | Low ceiling or short room leaves no space for a standard throw | Basements and single-car garages |
| **A computer that can run the software** | Simulation is a 3D rendering workload | Anyone assuming their existing laptop is fine |
| **Consistent lighting** | Camera-based units need controlled, stable light | Garages with a single overhead bulb |
| **Flooring** | Protects the slab, levels the mat, saves your joints | Bare concrete garages |
| **Side and ceiling netting** | Mishits leave the intended target area | Every room with a wall or a window near the hitting zone |
| **Power** | A dedicated run to the hitting corner | Detached garages and outbuildings |
| **Software renewal** | Subscriptions recur | Everyone, in year two |
| **Room work** | Insulation, a ceiling, drywall, a door that closes | Anyone who wants to use it in winter |

The list is not there to discourage you. It is there so that your budget survives contact with
your room. Our [space and budget planner](/tools/simulator-budget/) checks your dimensions
first, then splits your budget across the components in a defensible order.

## Working out your real cost per round

"Is it worth it" is not a price question. It is a rate question, and it has an arithmetic answer.

**Total cost of ownership over your first three years:**

```
Build cost
  + (annual software renewal × 3)
  + (consumables and replacement, mainly the mat and screen, × 3)
  + room work
  = three-year total
```

**Then divide by honest usage:**

```
three-year total ÷ (sessions per week × 50 weeks × 3)
  = your cost per session
```

Now compare that against what you currently pay for a range bucket, and what a round costs you
including the travel and the time. Two golfers with identical builds get wildly different
answers, and the variable is not the equipment. It is the number of sessions.

## Where to spend and where to save

If your budget is fixed, the order below reflects what changes your golf rather than what looks
impressive.

1. **Protect the mat budget.** It affects every swing you take and it affects your body.
2. **Buy the launch monitor your room can actually accommodate**, not the one with the best
   reviews. A unit that needs three feet behind the ball is worthless in a room that has two.
3. **Cut the screen and projector before you cut anything else.** A net build gives you the same
   data and the same repetitions for a fraction of the total.
4. **Do not cut the safety margin.** Side netting and ceiling clearance are not upgrades.
5. **Add the visual layer later.** Enclosure, screen and projector can all be retrofitted to a
   net build. A room that is too small cannot be retrofitted at all.

## A note on the prices you will find elsewhere

We do not publish component prices we have not verified ourselves, and we do not repeat figures
copied from other sites. This category moves quickly, launch monitor pricing in particular, and a
number that was accurate a year ago is now actively misleading — you can watch it happening on
competing guides that still carry a year in the title.

What we publish instead is the structure: the full component list, the costs that sit outside
package pricing, and a method for turning your own budget and your own room into a plan. Price
the components yourself against current listings, using the checklist above so that nothing gets
left out of the total.

When we have verified pricing to publish, it will appear here in a dated block that is refreshed
on a schedule, not baked into a headline.
