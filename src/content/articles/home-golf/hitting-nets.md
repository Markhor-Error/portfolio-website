A hitting net is a piece of safety equipment that happens to enable practice. That ordering is
not pedantry — it is the reason net setups go wrong. People size a net for the shot they intend
to hit, then hit a shot they did not intend, and the net was never in the way of it.

Everything below works from your worst shot rather than your average one.

## The advertised dimensions describe the target, not the risk

A net's stated size tells you how big the thing you are aiming at is. Useful, and not the number
that keeps a window intact.

Consider where the shots that miss actually go:

- **The shank.** Contact off the hosel leaves at a severe angle, close to right angles to the
  target line in the worst cases, and it leaves at speed. Nothing about a shank is slow or high.
  It is the shot that finds the wall beside you.
- **The thin.** A bladed iron climbs faster than you expect and can clear the top of a net sized
  by eye from a normal trajectory.
- **The top.** A topped ball skids along the floor at pace, passes underneath a net that stops
  short of the ground, and finds whatever is at ankle height on the far wall.
- **The heavy pull or push.** Not a mishit in the dramatic sense, just twenty degrees offline,
  which is enough to leave a target zone that was sized for ten.

The protected area you need is a **cone**, considerably wider and taller than the net panel you
are aiming at, and closed at the bottom. Side netting and overhead netting are not upgrades. They
are the parts that catch the shots the main panel was never in front of.

Stand where you intend to hit and look at everything the ball could reach: windows, the water
heater, the car, the doorway someone might walk through, the wall you are standing beside. If a
shank could reach it, the net has to be between it and you — or it has to move. Do this before
anything is installed, while both are still free.

## Rebound: why a tighter net is worse

The instinct is to tension a net drum-tight, because a tight net looks well made and a slack one
looks cheap.

A net stops a ball by absorbing its energy. A slack net has room to move, so it takes the energy
over a longer distance and the ball drops. A taut net has nowhere to go, so it returns a
meaningful fraction of that energy — and the ball comes back at you, quickly, from a position you
are not watching because you are in your follow-through.

Slack has a limit in the other direction, since a net with too much give can let a ball reach
whatever is behind it. The right answer is the manufacturer's stated tension and the
manufacturer's stated distance, both of which exist because of this trade-off, and neither of
which should be tightened by eye because it looks better.

## What actually fails

Inspect the places that take the load, not the place that takes the ball.

1. **The seams.** Where panels are joined is where repeated shock loading concentrates. A seam
   opening up is the most common real failure, and it starts small.
2. **The attachment points.** Wherever the net meets the frame — bungees, clips, ties — is a
   stress concentration and usually the cheapest part of the assembly.
3. **The frame joints.** Particularly on collapsible frames that get put up and taken down. Look
   for elongated holes, loosening fasteners, and any joint that has developed play.
4. **The netting itself**, last. Sunlight degrades some materials over time, which matters for a
   net that lives in a bright garage or outdoors, but an intact panel is rarely the thing that
   lets a ball through.

Put the inspection on a schedule rather than a suspicion. Once a month, or after any session
where something sounded wrong, takes two minutes.

## Assembly details that matter more than the spec

- **Close the bottom gap.** A net that stops several inches above the floor is a letterbox for
  topped shots. Weight it, extend it, or run a skirt.
- **Check what is behind it.** A net absorbs most of the energy, not all of it, and something
  will occasionally reach the wall behind. Do not position a net directly in front of drywall you
  care about, glass, or anything electrical.
- **Watch the floor for balls.** A returned ball sitting behind you is a fall risk that people
  discover mid-swing. A ball tray or a sloped mat surround is a genuine safety feature.
- **Think about who else is in the room.** The most serious risk in a home setup is not the wall.
  It is somebody walking into the hitting zone from a door behind you while you are mid-backswing.
  A closed door, a visible barrier, or a rule everyone in the house knows.

## A net setup is a complete answer

There is a habit of describing a net build as the version you have before you can afford a real
simulator. It is worth pushing back on.

A net, a good [hitting mat](/home-golf/hitting-mats/) and a launch monitor give you every number
a screen build gives you, the same repetitions, in a fraction of the space and at a fraction of
the total. What the screen and projector add is the picture and simulated course play — which is
genuinely enjoyable and genuinely motivating, and which is unrelated to whether your ball
striking improves.

If the budget is tight, a net setup built properly beats a screen setup built badly. And since
an enclosure and screen can be retrofitted to a correctly measured room later, starting with a
net costs you nothing but the picture.
