Most home simulator builds go wrong in the same order. Someone reads reviews, chooses a launch
monitor, buys it, and then discovers that the room it is going into cannot give the device what
its technology needs. By then the money is spent and the options are bad.

The fix is unglamorous. Measure first, shortlist second, buy third. This page covers what to
measure, why each dimension constrains a different part of the build, and the order to assemble
things in so that the reversible decisions come after the irreversible ones.

## The three dimensions constrain three different things

A room is not "big enough" or "too small". Each dimension gates something specific, and knowing
which is which tells you what a shortfall actually costs you.

| Dimension | What it gates | What happens if it is short |
|---|---|---|
| **Ceiling height** | Whether you can make a full swing at all | Nothing fixes it. The build is limited to shorter clubs, or it is not a simulator |
| **Depth** | Which launch monitor technologies can work | Narrows you to specific sensor types, often the more expensive ones |
| **Width** | Safety margin and whether you can host both handednesses | Mishits reach walls; a second player of the other hand may not fit |

Height is the one that ends projects, so start there.

### Ceiling height is a calculation, not a number

Published minimums are guesses about an average golfer. Yours depends on how tall you are and how
upright you swing. The clearance you need is set by the highest point the clubhead reaches, which
occurs at the top of the backswing and again through the finish — and at both of those moments
the club is above your hands, which are already above your head.

So the working estimate is:

```
your height
  + your arms extended overhead
  + the length of the longest club you intend to swing
  + a margin you will not regret
```

Do not settle this with arithmetic alone. **Take a driver into the room and make ten full,
committed swings**, including the follow-through, which is where most ceiling strikes happen. A
tentative half-swing in a low room proves nothing except that you were being careful.

Then look up properly. The number that matters is not the ceiling — it is the lowest thing in the
swing arc. Light fittings, joists, ducting, sprinkler heads, and in a garage, the door track and
the opener motor, all sit below the ceiling and all are the actual limit.

### Depth is a sensor question

How much room you need in front of and behind the ball is decided by the launch monitor
technology, not by your swing. Radar units observe the ball in flight and need clear space in
front of it; camera units read the moments around impact and need a defined position beside,
above or behind it. That is the whole reason the sensor decision cannot be made from reviews —
the room decides which technologies are even eligible.

The [launch monitor guide](/home-golf/launch-monitors/) covers the technologies and the five
questions to ask about any specific unit.

Beyond the sensor, you need enough distance from the ball to the net or screen for the ball to
have slowed and for the rebound to be controlled. Closer is not simply worse, but closer is
faster and harder on everything, including you.

### Width is a safety question

Width gets ignored because a golf swing is narrow. The shots are not. A shanked wedge leaves at a
severe angle and does so at speed, and a room that comfortably fits your swing may put a wall,
a window or a water heater in the path of a bad one.

Width also decides whether the space can serve a left-hander and a right-hander without moving
the hitting station, which matters more than people expect once the setup exists and other people
want to use it.

## Build in this order

The order below is not about convenience. It puts the decisions you cannot undo first and the
ones you can retrofit last.

1. **Measure, and swing in the space.** Height, then depth, then width, then the obstructions
   below the ceiling. Write the numbers down.
2. **Fix the room before anything goes into it.** Lighting, power, flooring and, if the room needs
   it, insulation and a ceiling. All of this is far cheaper with an empty floor.
3. **Choose the launch monitor the room can host.** Filter by technology fit first, then by the
   metrics you actually intend to use.
4. **Buy the best hitting mat the budget allows.** It affects every swing and, over time, your
   wrists and elbows. See [hitting mats](/home-golf/hitting-mats/).
5. **Add the net or enclosure, with the side and overhead protection.** See
   [hitting nets](/home-golf/hitting-nets/) for the safety margins.
6. **Then the screen, projector and software**, in whatever order the budget allows.

Steps one and two are the ones people skip, and they are the only two that get more expensive
the longer you wait.

## Where the ball goes when you miss

Package listings show the shot going where it was aimed. Plan for the ones that do not.

- **Above the target.** A thin shot climbs fast. Overhead netting between the hitting position
  and the target is not an accessory.
- **To the sides.** Shanks and heavy pulls leave at angles the enclosure was not designed to
  catch. Side netting extends the catchable cone.
- **Straight back.** Balls rebound. A screen tensioned tight and hung close returns the ball at
  you with real speed, which is a reason to respect the recommended distance and to think about
  where you are standing.
- **Down.** A topped ball skids along the floor at pace and finds whatever is at ankle height on
  the far wall.

The general rule: protect a cone that is considerably wider than the one you think you need, and
assume your worst shot rather than your average one.

## The net build is a real destination

There is an assumption that a net setup is what you have until you can afford a screen. It is not
a lesser build, and for a lot of golfers it is the better one.

A launch monitor, a good mat and a net give you every number a full simulator would give you, the
same repetitions, and a far smaller footprint. What the screen and projector add is the picture
and the ability to play courses — genuinely enjoyable, genuinely motivating, and unrelated to
whether your swing gets better.

If the budget is tight, building a net setup properly beats building a screen setup badly. And
because the enclosure, screen and projector can all be added later to a room that was measured
correctly, nothing is lost by starting there. What cannot be added later is ceiling height.
