The hitting mat is the least exciting line in a simulator budget and the one that most affects
your practice. It is under every swing you take, it decides what your strike feels like, and if
it is the wrong one it will quietly teach you a fault while making your elbows hurt.

It is also the component that gets cut when the budget runs short, because a mat looks like a
mat. The differences are real, they are mechanical, and you can test for them.

## The two things a bad mat does to you

They are independent problems. A mat can have one without the other, and the cheapest mats have
both.

### 1. It hides your worst strike

This is the important one, and it is not a matter of degree.

When you strike the ground behind the ball on turf, the club digs in. Grass and soil absorb
energy, the clubhead slows, and the shot comes out short and heavy. The feedback is immediate and
unambiguous: you were behind it.

Do the same thing on a thin mat over a hard base and the physics change completely. The club
cannot dig, so it **bounces**. The leading edge skips off the surface and rises into the back of
the ball, and the ball comes off with reasonable speed and a flight you would accept. Your low
point was in exactly the wrong place and the shot rewarded you for it.

> A fat strike is the most common ball-striking fault in amateur golf. A cheap mat is a machine
> for concealing it.

The consequence is not that your simulator numbers are slightly optimistic. It is that you can
practise for a whole winter, build confidence, take the swing to a course in spring and find that
it does not work — because you have been rehearsing a low point the mat was covering for.

### 2. It sends the shock back into you

Turf yields. A firm surface over concrete does not, so the energy that would have gone into the
ground comes back up the shaft and into your hands, wrists and elbows.

This is a volume problem as much as a hardness problem. A firm mat used lightly is a minor issue.
A firm mat used for a few hundred balls a week is a repeated impact load on joints that were not
built for it, and the golfers who find out the hard way are the ones who finally had a setup and
used it enthusiastically.

Two things reduce it: a mat that lets the club pass through the surface with resistance rather
than rebounding off it, and a shock-absorbing layer between the mat and the slab.

## How to test a mat rather than read about it

Marketing language for mats is unregulated. "Tour quality", "true turf feel" and similar phrases
have no agreed definition and no measurement behind them. These tests do.

1. **The deliberate fat shot.** Aim to strike an inch or two behind the ball on purpose. On a good
   mat the club meets resistance and the shot suffers. On a bad one the club skids and the ball
   goes surprisingly well. This single test tells you most of what you need to know.
2. **Push the club through the surface with your hands.** You are feeling for whether the fibres
   part and let the sole travel, or whether the head sits on top and slides.
3. **Press the base with your thumb.** You are checking what is under the fibres. A dense
   backing over nothing is a different product from one with a genuine cushioning layer.
4. **Look for the wear strip.** On any mat that has been used, wear concentrates in a small area.
   How quickly the surface flattens there is the useful indicator of life, and it is visible on a
   demo mat in a shop.

## The fitting details that decide whether you use it

These do not appear on product pages and they determine whether the setup is pleasant enough to
use four times a week.

- **Height matching.** If the mat sits proud of the surrounding floor and you stand off it, you
  are effectively playing every shot from a lie above your feet. Either stand on the mat or bring
  the surrounding floor up to its level. Ball-above-feet is a swing change you did not ask for.
- **Mat creep.** A heavy mat on a smooth slab still moves under repeated strikes, and a mat that
  has shifted two inches changes your ball position without telling you. Check whether it needs
  anchoring or a high-friction underlay.
- **Hitting position rotation.** Wear concentrates where you stand. Moving your position around
  the mat and rotating the mat periodically extends its usable life considerably, which is worth
  building into the layout from the start.
- **What is underneath.** On a garage slab this matters as much as the mat itself. A shock layer
  underneath changes both the feel and the load on your joints.

## Budget it as a consumable

A mat wears. The fibres in the strip you hit from flatten, the surface gets faster and harder,
and the mat becomes progressively more of the thing described at the top of this page. Volume
decides the timescale.

That makes a mat a **running cost**, not a one-time build cost, and it belongs in the three-year
total you calculate in the [cost guide](/home-golf/simulator-cost/) rather than in the initial
purchase alone. Budgeting for it once and being surprised later is a common and avoidable
mistake.

## Where the mat sits in the build

If your budget is under pressure, this is the component to defend. The
[build guide](/home-golf/simulators/) puts the mat above the screen and projector for a simple
reason: the screen changes what your practice looks like, and the mat changes what your practice
teaches you.
