Launch monitor marketing presents a long list of numbers and implies that all of them are
measurements. They are not. Every unit on the market measures a few things directly and
calculates everything else from a model, and the boundary between those two groups is the single
most useful thing you can know about any device.

It is also the thing you have to dig for. The measured list is on the front of the box. The
modelled list is in a footnote, if it is anywhere.

## Measured versus modelled

A launch monitor observes a physical event that lasts a few thousandths of a second. Whatever it
cannot observe, it infers.

- A **measured** value comes from the sensor. The device saw it happen.
- A **modelled** value is calculated from measured values plus assumptions — about the ball, the
  strike, the air, and how those normally relate.

Modelling is not cheating, and a well-built model is genuinely useful. The problem is that both
kinds of number appear in the same font, in the same list, to the same decimal place. Nothing on
the screen tells you which one you are looking at.

largest on mishits, partial swings and unusual strikes — which is exactly when you are trying to

reliable. It just becomes wrong, quietly.

## The two technologies, and what each one needs from your room

This is where the decision actually gets made, and it has nothing to do with review scores.

### Radar

Radar units track the ball after it leaves the club by reading the frequency shift of a signal
bouncing off it. Because they observe the flight rather than the impact, they need to **see a
usable portion of that flight** — which means clear space in front of the ball.

That requirement is the one most often broken in a home build. A radar unit designed around
outdoor use, placed in a garage with a net eight feet away, is being asked to do its job with
most of its evidence removed. Some units handle short indoor distances well; this is a specific
question to ask about a specific unit, not an assumption to make.

### Cameras (photometric)

Camera systems take very fast images in a narrow window around impact and derive everything from
what they see there. They need less room in front of the ball, which is why they dominate tight
indoor builds — but they need a **defined position** beside, above or behind the ball, and they
need **stable, adequate light**.

A camera unit in a garage lit by one overhead bulb, with sunlight moving across the floor as the
door stands open, is working against its own requirements. Lighting is a real line item in a
camera-based build, and it belongs in the budget from the start.

### The constraint above both

Ceiling height decides whether you can swing a driver at all, and no sensor technology fixes a
room that is too short. Measure that first, then room depth, then width. Our
[space and budget planner](/tools/simulator-budget/) does this arithmetic against your own
dimensions and tells you which technologies your room can actually host.

## Match the metric to the question you are asking

Buying more metrics than you need is the most common way to overspend here. Work backwards from
what you want to change.

| What you want to work on | The metric that answers it | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Real carry distances for club gapping | Carry distance, and its spread | The single most useful number for most golfers |
| Wedge distance control | Carry plus spin | Spin modelling is weakest on partial shots, so measurement earns its cost here |
| Shot shape and curvature | Club path and face angle | Without both, you are guessing at the relationship that bends the ball |
| Strike quality | Impact location, smash factor | Some units measure strike location directly; many infer it |
| Driver optimisation | Launch angle, spin rate, ball speed | The classic three, and the case where spin accuracy matters most |
| Playing simulated courses | Ball speed, launch, direction | The bar is lower than for practice; the simulation smooths over a lot |

If your honest answer is "carry distances and consistency", you need substantially less device
than the marketing assumes. That is a real saving, and it frees budget for the
[mat](/home-golf/hitting-mats/), which affects every swing you take.

## Five questions to ask about any unit

Run these against the product page before you shortlist. They are answerable, and the answers
separate units far better than a star rating does.

1. **Which values does this unit measure directly, and which does it calculate?** If the page
   will not say plainly, that is itself informative.
2. **How much space does it need in front of, behind and beside the ball?** Compare against your
   actual measurements, not your estimate of them.
3. **What are its lighting requirements?** Especially for camera systems, and especially in a
   garage.
4. **Does it require marked balls, stickers or a specific ball model** to hit its stated accuracy?
   This is a recurring cost and a recurring inconvenience, and it is usually in a footnote.
5. **Which simulation software does it work with, and is that a subscription?** Software lock-in
   is decided by the hardware you buy, and discovered later by people who did not ask.

## What a launch monitor will not do

It will not tell you why. It gives you the *what* — the numbers describing what happened — and
the interpretation is still yours. A unit reporting a path six degrees left and a face two
degrees left has described a slice precisely without suggesting a single thing to do about it.

That gap is worth naming, because it is where a lot of expensive equipment goes quiet. Numbers
change behaviour only when you know which number to move and in which direction. If you are still
working out what your ball flight is telling you, our
[ball flight diagnostic](/tools/ball-flight-diagnostic/) covers the interpretation side for free,
and it is worth doing before you spend anything at all.

## Where this leaves the decision

Shortlist by room first, then by the metrics you actually intend to use, and only then by price.
Doing it in that order removes most of the market before you have read a single review, and the
units that survive are the ones that can physically work where you are putting them.
