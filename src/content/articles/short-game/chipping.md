Ask ten golfers how they chip and you will get ten answers, and several of them will come from the
same golfer depending on the lie. That is the problem. Chipping is the easiest part of golf to
build a repeatable method for, and most amateurs instead own a menu of half-remembered techniques
and no confidence in any of them.

This page gives you one setup, one motion, and a way of choosing the club. The technique does not
change. The club does.

## The one fault behind almost every bad chip

Before technique, the mechanism. Chunks and thins feel like opposite mistakes — one goes nowhere,
one goes miles — and golfers treat them as two problems. They are one problem.

**Your low point is behind the ball.**

The club is descending, reaches its lowest point, then begins to rise. If that lowest point happens
before the ball:

- The club meets the ground first. That is the **chunk**.
- Or it bounces off the turf and is already rising by the time it reaches the ball, so the leading
  edge catches it above centre. That is the **thin**.

Same fault, two outcomes, decided by turf and luck. Which is why fixing your thin by "trying to hit
down more" and fixing your chunk by "trying to pick it clean" sends you round in circles.

> Move the low point in front of the ball and both shots disappear together.

Everything below is built to do that.

## The setup that does most of the work

Get these four things right and the motion becomes easy.

### 1. Narrow your stance

Feet close together, closer than for a full shot. You are not generating power and a wide base
encourages weight to move around. Narrow keeps you still.

### 2. Weight on the lead foot, and keep it there

Around 60 to 70 percent on your lead side at address, and — this is the part people miss — **it
stays there**. Do not shift back and forward. Weight moving backwards in the downswing is the most
direct route to a low point behind the ball.

### 3. Ball back of centre, hands ahead

Play the ball slightly back of the middle of your narrow stance, with your hands a touch ahead of
it so the shaft leans toward the target. That shaft lean is what puts the low point in front of the
ball, which is the whole point.

### 4. Grip down and stand closer

Choke down on the handle and let your arms hang. You are shortening the club, which gives you more
control over where the bottom of the arc is.

## The motion

Once the setup is right, the motion is almost nothing.

**Rock the shoulders. Keep the wrists quiet. Accelerate through.**

Your arms and the club form a shape at address, and that shape holds while your shoulders rock it
back and through. The wrists keep the angle they started with. There is no hinge, no flick, no
scoop.

Two things to watch:

- **Backswing length sets distance.** Not hand speed. Set the length, then swing through at a
  constant tempo.
- **Follow through at least as far as you went back.** A stroke that stops at the ball is a
  decelerating stroke, and deceleration ruins contact more reliably than any technical fault.

If you find yourself decelerating, your backswing was too long and your body knew it. Make it
shorter than feels correct and commit.

## Change the club, not the swing

Here is where you gain strokes without gaining skill.

The ball is more predictable rolling on the green than flying through the air. So the objective is
to land it on the green as soon as safely possible and let it run to the hole. That means using the
**straightest-faced club the situation allows**.

- **Lots of green between you and the flag** — a 7, 8 or 9 iron. It lands early and runs.
- **A moderate amount of green** — a pitching wedge or gap wedge.
- **Short-sided, or a bunker or slope to carry** — a sand or lob wedge, because now you need the
  flight.

Same setup, same motion, different club. That is the entire system.

### Build your own carry-to-roll numbers

Published ratios for each club are averages of other people's wedges, lies and greens, and they
will not match yours. Ten minutes gets you the real ones.

1. Pick a landing spot on the practice green and mark it.
2. Chip ten balls to it with your 9 iron, using the same setup and a backswing you can repeat.
3. Note roughly how far past the spot they finish.
4. Repeat with your pitching wedge, then your sand wedge.

You now have three clubs whose behaviour you know. Pick the club by where you need the ball to land
and how far it then has to travel — rather than picking a club and hoping.

## Where chipping stops and pitching starts

A useful boundary, because past it this technique no longer applies.

A **chip** is a short shot with a quiet, wrist-free motion, spending most of its life on the
ground. Once the shot needs enough length that your wrists have to hinge and the ball spends more
time in the air than on the turf, you are **pitching** — a different motion with different faults.

The technique on this page is deliberately limited to the greenside chip. Trying to stretch it to
40 yards is where golfers start inventing the six methods this page exists to replace.

## What to practise, in order

1. **Contact first.** Fifteen balls, one club, one landing spot. You are looking for a small divot
   *after* the ball, and consistent strike.
2. **Then landing spot control.** Same club, three different spots, until you can land it where you
   choose.
3. **Then club selection.** Same spot, three clubs, watching the roll change.

Do not move to step two until step one is reliable. Most golfers practising chipping are quietly
practising step three with a strike they cannot repeat, which teaches them nothing.
