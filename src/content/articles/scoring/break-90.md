If you are shooting in the 90s, the advice that got you here has run out. Removing disasters was
the whole job at the last milestone, and you have largely done it — your cards do not have many
sevens on them any more.

What is left is different. You are now reaching a lot of holes in position to make bogey or better
and not converting them. Breaking 90 is about the shots between 120 yards and the hole, plus a tee
policy you actually stick to.

## The arithmetic

On a par 72, breaking 90 means 89, which is 17 over.

- **Bogey on all eighteen** is 90. One shot short.
- **One par, seventeen bogeys** is 89. You are there.
- **Four pars, thirteen bogeys, one double** is 89.
- **Three pars, fourteen bogeys, one double** is 90. Agonisingly close.

Two things fall out of this that are worth sitting with.

First, **you do not need to play well.** Bogey golf plus one par breaks 90. Nobody needs a good
round.

Second, **each double bogey now costs you a par to cancel out.** At the last milestone doubles were
acceptable. Here they are expensive, because pars are hard to come by and doubles are easy.

## Where the strokes actually are

Three places, in order of what they are worth to most golfers at this level.

### 1. Approach shots inside 150 yards

This is the biggest single pot. You will face six to ten of these a round, and the difference
between finishing 20 feet away and finishing in a bunker is a stroke every time.

Two changes:

- **Take enough club.** Amateurs miss short far more often than long, because they select clubs
  based on their best ever strike. Front bunkers and false fronts collect these shots. Take one
  more and swing smoothly.
- **Aim at the fat part of the green.** Not the pin. The centre, or the safe side away from the
  trouble. A 30-foot putt is a good outcome.

### 2. Chipping

Almost everyone at this level loses strokes here, and it compounds: a chip that finishes 25 feet
away creates the long putt that becomes a three-putt. Fix the chip and you remove two strokes, not
one.

The single technique in our [chipping guide](/short-game/chipping/) is deliberately one method,
because the six-methods-and-trust-none problem is what costs strokes here.

### 3. Putting from long range

Three putts from outside 30 feet are almost always speed, not line. The first putt's job is to
finish inside a circle around the hole, not to go in.

## Par 5s: the third shot is the scoring shot

Par 5s are where the difference between 89 and 93 usually lives, and the common instinct is exactly
wrong.

Trying to reach in two brings a long club into play near a green — and a long club missed near a
green means a bunker, thick rough or an awkward chip, which is how par 5s become doubles.

Instead, treat the hole as **three comfortable shots**, and plan backwards from a wedge distance you
like:

1. Tee shot in play.
2. Second shot to leave your favourite wedge yardage — often deliberately shorter than you could
   hit.
3. A wedge from a distance you have practised, to the middle of the green.

That is a realistic par and a very likely bogey. It also removes the doubles.

The exception is a genuinely open par 5 where a missed long shot finishes on grass with a
straightforward chip. Then go.

## Par 3s: play the yardage, not the pin

Par 3s produce more doubles per hole than anything else, because there is no recovery shot built in
— you are on the green or you are chipping.

- **Take the club for the back of the green**, not for the pin. Long is almost always safer than
  short.
- **Ignore a tucked flag entirely.** Middle of the green.
- **Note where the trouble is and miss on the other side.** Deciding that before you swing is most
  of the work.

## The tee: pick a strategy and follow it

By now your tee shot is probably serviceable. What costs you is inconsistency of *decision* rather
than of strike — driver on the wrong hole, once a round, at exactly the wrong moment.

Before you play, decide your policy for each hole and stick to it:

- **Driver** where a miss on either side is still playable.
- **A club you can find** where there is water, out of bounds, or trees on the side you tend to
  miss.
- **Never** the club you have never trusted, chosen because the hole is long.

### Play your bias instead of fighting it

If your stock shot moves left to right, aim down the left side and let it work back. If it moves
right to left, aim right.

A repeatable shot with a known curve is more valuable than a straight one you cannot produce on
demand. Golfers stuck in the 90s frequently aim straight at every target and then hope today is the
day the ball goes straight.

This is a scoring decision, not a swing change. If your ball movement is genuinely too much to play
with — a full slice or hook rather than a shape — that belongs in [swing fixes](/fixes/) and is
worth fixing on its own terms.

## Find your real wedge distances

This is the highest-value practice hour available to you, and almost nobody does it.

Published wedge distances are other people's. Yours are what matters, and you probably do not know
them.

1. Take your pitching wedge, gap wedge and sand wedge to a range with measured targets or a field
   you can pace.
2. Hit ten shots with each at a **smooth, three-quarter, repeatable** swing — not full effort.
3. Ignore the best one. Write down the distance the middle of the group carried.
4. Do the same again with a shorter, controlled swing.

You now have six known distances instead of three hopeful ones. That is what makes the par-5 layup
plan above work, and it turns the "between clubs" problem into a decision rather than a guess.

## What to stop doing

- **Stop aiming at flags.** The centre of the green is the target until you are much better.
- **Stop trying to reach par 5s in two** unless the miss is harmless.
- **Stop taking the club that would be perfect if struck perfectly.** Take the one that is fine
  when struck normally.
- **Stop practising only the driver.** You will hit fourteen drivers and around forty shots from
  inside 120 yards.

## Once you get there

Breaking 90 comes from converting the positions you are already reaching. **Breaking 80 is a
different problem again**: avoiding mistakes is no longer enough, and you have to start actually
making birdies and converting the chances you create.

If you are still writing down the occasional triple, [how to break 100](/scoring/break-100/) covers
the disaster-removal work that has to come first — it is worth re-reading even from the 90s, because
one blow-up hole is the difference between 89 and 93.
