Almost everything written about breaking 100 is swing advice, and that is why it does not work.
The golfer shooting 104 does not need a better swing. They already hit enough acceptable shots to
break 100 comfortably — they just also hit two or three holes that cost eight strokes between them.

Breaking 100 is subtraction. This page is about what to subtract.

## The arithmetic, so you know what you are aiming at

On a par 72, breaking 100 means 99 or better, which is 27 over.

- **Bogey on every hole** is 90. That is six shots better than you need.
- **Bogey on fourteen holes and double bogey on four** is 94. Still comfortable.
- **Bogey on fifteen and double on three** is 93.

So the target is not "play well". It is: **bogey most holes, and never worse than double.**

Look at your last card with that in mind. You will almost certainly find that you already made
bogey or better on most holes, and that the round was destroyed by two or three numbers you would
rather not write down. Those holes are the whole gap.

## Where the disasters come from

Blow-up holes have a small number of causes, and none of them is a bad swing on its own. A bad
swing costs one shot. A disaster needs a bad swing followed by a bad decision.

1. **A tee shot out of play.** Penalty, or an unplayable position, and the hole is already gone.
2. **The hero recovery.** In the trees, a four-foot gap, a 3 iron, and a plan to reach the green.
   It hits a trunk. Now you are in the trees again, one shot worse off.
3. **Repeated failed escapes.** Three attempts to advance the ball out of a bad lie when one
   sideways chip would have ended it.
4. **The greenside gamble.** Short-sided over a bunker to a tight pin, trying a shot you have never
   practised, leaving it in the sand, doing it again.
5. **Three putts from long range**, usually because the first putt was struck to hole rather than to
   finish close.

Notice what these have in common. Only the first involves a golf swing failing. The rest are
decisions made *after* something went wrong — and that is where the strokes actually go.

## The rule that changes your scores this weekend

One rule. It is worth more than any tip on this page.

> **When something goes wrong, the next shot's only job is to get you back in play.**

Not to recover the hole. Not to save par. Not to make up the lost shot. Back in play, in the short
grass, with a clear swing at the target.

That means:

- Out of position in trees? The shot is **sideways to the fairway**. Not forward-and-through-a-gap.
  Sideways.
- Bad lie in thick rough? The club is the one that gets it *out*, in one attempt, wherever that
  ends up.
- Behind a tree? Under it, around it, or backwards — whichever needs no miracle.

You will make bogey or double. You were going to make double anyway. What you have avoided is the
seven.

The hardest part is not the technique. It is accepting that the hole is already partly lost and
refusing to spend more on it.

## Off the tee: take less club than you want

The driver is the club that turns bogeys into triples, because it puts the ball furthest offline.

At this level, a tee shot that finishes 190 yards away in the fairway is worth substantially more
than one that finishes 240 yards away in trouble — because the second one does not have a next
shot, it has a rescue.

**Use the driver on holes where a bad one still leaves you playable.** Wide holes, no water, no out
of bounds. Everywhere else, take the club you can keep in play — a 5 wood, a hybrid, even a long
iron. Yes, you will have a longer second shot. A longer second shot from short grass is a better
position than a shorter one from a bush.

This single change removes most of category one above.

## Approach shots: aim at the middle, always

Pins are placed to tempt you into the trouble beside them. At this stage you have no business
looking at them.

**Aim at the centre of the green on every approach.** The middle is the largest target, it is
furthest from every hazard, and a 30-foot putt is a completely fine outcome.

And be honest about the club. Most amateurs quote their best-ever distance with a club and then
select it as though that is what it does. Take one more club than you think, swing at a comfortable
pace, and let the ball land pin-high rather than in the front bunker.

## Inside 100 yards: lay up to a distance you like

This is the counterintuitive one, and it is worth several strokes a round.

You are 90 yards out with a bunker guarding a front pin. The instinct is to go at it. But a 90-yard
wedge over sand to a tight pin is a shot with a genuine chance of finishing in the bunker, from
where you may need two more.

Instead: play to the wide part of the green, or lay up to the distance you actually hit well. Two
putts from 40 feet is a five. Two bunker shots and two putts is a seven.

The same applies around the green. Our [chipping guide](/short-game/chipping/) covers the one
technique to use, and the principle behind it — get it on the green and let it roll — is exactly
the principle that keeps big numbers off your card.

## Putting: the first putt has one job

Three putts are quiet score-killers and they are almost always a distance problem, not a line
problem.

From outside 30 feet, **the first putt's job is to finish close, not to go in.** Pick a spot the
size of a dustbin lid around the hole and try to leave the ball inside it. You will three-putt far
less, and you will hole a few by accident.

Inside six feet, take your time and commit to a line. These are the putts that make the difference
between the bogey you planned and the double you did not.

## What not to worry about yet

To be clear about what this page is *not* telling you to do:

- **Do not rebuild your swing.** You have enough swing to shoot in the 90s today.
- **Do not buy clubs.** Nothing in a shop removes a triple bogey.
- **Do not learn specialist shots.** The flop shot is not why you are shooting 104.

If your ball flight is genuinely unplayable — a slice that reaches another fairway every time —
that is worth fixing, and our [swing fixes](/fixes/) cover it. But fix it because the ball is out
of play, not because you think better technique is the route to the number. It is not. Fewer
disasters is.

## Once you get there

Breaking 100 is about removing disasters. **Breaking 90 is a different job** — by then the
disasters are mostly gone, and the strokes come from converting chances and from a short game that
holds up. [How to break 90](/scoring/break-90/) picks up exactly where this page ends.
