Clubs & Gear

Golf Equipment Guides: What to Buy, and When Not To

Equipment can genuinely help. It cannot fix a swing fault, and the industry has a strong commercial interest in blurring that line. These guides try not to.

ME

Written by Going Eagle Editorial Team · Reviewed by Mike Evans, Golf Coach — 6 years of coaching experience

Read this before you spend anything

Three things are true at the same time, and most gear content only admits the first:

  • Modern equipment is genuinely better than equipment from fifteen years ago, particularly in forgiveness on off-centre strikes.
  • Fitting matters more than model. A well-fitted mid-range club will outperform a poorly-fitted premium one, consistently.
  • Equipment cannot fix a swing fault. A draw-biased driver reduces a slice. It does not cure the out-to-in path producing it, and the fault will reappear the moment you change clubs.

Our bias, stated openly

We earn commission when you buy through our links, which gives us an obvious incentive to tell you to buy things. We manage it by publishing how we test, labelling the evidence behind every recommendation, and saying plainly when the answer is to keep what you own. Judge us against that.

Where the money is best spent

If you have a fixed budget and want the most strokes per pound, this is roughly the order that works for a mid-handicap golfer — and it is not the order the industry advertises.

  1. A fitting, even for clubs you already own. Lie angle and shaft alone can change ball flight substantially.
  2. Wedges, if yours are worn. Grooves lose spin, and worn wedges cost you short-game control every round.
  3. A putter that fits your stroke, which is a fitting question rather than a price question.
  4. Golf balls matched to your swing speed. The cheapest meaningful change available.
  5. A driver, last. It is the most expensive club and the one you hit fourteen times a round.
